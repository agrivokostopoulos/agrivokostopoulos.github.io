Aristeidis Grivokostopoulos

Why do some fiscal crises break patronage networks while others leave them intact? When do political institutions shape financial outcomes, and when do markets ignore politics entirely?

I study sovereign debt and political institutions across the first age of globalisation, when international capital flows reshaped what it meant to be a creditworthy state. My work asks how peripheral borrowers navigated access to London's capital markets, how parliamentary coalitions formed and dissolved under fiscal pressure, and what happens when models developed in one setting travel to another.

About

I am a second-year PhD student in the Department of Economic History at the London School of Economics, funded by the ESRC. My supervisors are Albrecht Ritschl and Olivier Accominotti.

My research examines sovereign debt, financial markets, and political institutions across the long 19th century and the first age of globalisation, with a focus on how fiscal crises reshape legislative coalitions and borrowing costs. I combine archival research with computational text analysis, network methods, and panel econometrics. I also work on applied AI and labour-market research through the LSE Data Science Institute and the Hellenic Observatory. Before academia, I worked in venture capital (Creandum, Speedinvest) and operations (Clausematch, Genie).

Working Paper

Made in Greece or Echoed from Europe?

Sentiment and Awareness Among International Partners During the 1931–1932 Greek Sovereign Debt Crisis

How did international perceptions of Greece shift in the months before the 1932 default? Using a novel LLM-based text-as-data approach on the Tsouderos Archive — private correspondence and memoranda of the Bank of Greece Governor — I map sentiment and topic trends in communications with foreign officials. The analysis reveals a clear shift towards pessimism from June 1931, preceding and intensifying after the Sterling Crisis of September 1931, even against heightened anxieties in the Greek press. An SVAR model shows a significant negative correlation between archive sentiment and Greek government bond yields. Change point analysis identifies two structural breaks (June and September 1931) and a shift in topic composition in November 1931 towards debt relief and financial assistance.

Working Paper

From War Finance to Fiscal Supervision

Seniority Structures and Debt Restructuring in the Ottoman Empire, Egypt, and Greece, 1870–1907

Compares OPDA, Caisse de la Dette, and IFC restructurings using a bond-level London Stock Exchange panel of 47 securities. The paper estimates junior-senior yield spreads around creditor supervision: Ottoman and Egyptian fixed-coupon cases fit the Ho-Ritschl (2024) seniority-reversal channel, while Greece's state-contingent coupon design generates spread inversion, a wrong-sign issuance effect, and faster exit from default-like yields.

Working Paper

Permeable Patronage

Fiscal Crises and Domain-Specific Defection in the Greek Parliament, 1875–1905

Builds a new roll-call dataset from digitised Hellenic Parliament proceedings: 755 nominal roll calls, 132,246 MP-vote observations, 1,049 matched MPs, and 90.5% policy-domain coverage. The paper shows patronage discipline remained the default but became permeable at constituency-targeting fiscal flash points after default, and formalises the mechanism through a π-pattern/π-binding decomposition.

Working Paper

Between Civilisation and Constitution

Greek Parliamentary Debates on Gold Standard Adoption, 1875–1885

Reconstructs monetary-policy debates from 988 parliamentary session files, including 409 sessions matching a narrow monetary vocabulary. The paper argues that critics accurately identified fiscal, distributional, regional, gradualist, and constitutional risks, but failed to assemble a counter-model to gold; it maps thirteen parliamentarians across argumentative registers and explains gold's authority as narrative embedding rather than technical proof alone.

Working Paper

Sterling by Design

Contractual Standardisation and Currency Dominance on the London Stock Exchange, 1869–1929

Uses a security-level panel of 957 sovereign debt instruments from the Investors' Monthly Manual. The paper shows sterling's share of income flows rose from 79 to 99 per cent as multi-currency clauses, bullion clauses, and non-sterling denominations disappeared; Bai-Perron breaks in the late 1870s-1880s precede periphery gold adoption; captive borrowers paid a yield premium over outside-option borrowers.

Working Paper

Why Did Depositors Run?

Withdrawals in the German Banking Crisis of 1931 (with Philip Fliers)

Why were bank runs so pervasive in 1931, and why did depositors withdraw heavily from banks that survived? Using bimonthly balance sheet data from 1925–1935, we show that severe deposit withdrawals were widespread, highly predictable up to two years before the crisis, and differed systematically across maturities. Withdrawals were accompanied by substantial reallocation of deposits across banks.
