Aristeidis Grivokostopoulos

PhD Candidate • LSE Economic History

Why do some fiscal crises break patronage networks while others leave them intact? When do political institutions shape financial outcomes, and when do markets ignore politics entirely?

I study sovereign debt and political institutions across the first age of globalisation, when international capital flows reshaped what it meant to be a creditworthy state. My work asks how peripheral borrowers managed their access to London's capital markets, how parliamentary coalitions formed and dissolved under fiscal pressure, and what happens when models developed in one setting travel to another.

About

I am a third-year PhD student in the Department of Economic History at the London School of Economics, funded by an ESRC Doctoral Training Partnership award to September 2028. My supervisors are Albrecht Ritschl and Olivier Accominotti. My thesis is “Hard Money, Soft Sovereignty: Peripheral Debt, Supervision, and Parliamentary Politics during the First Globalisation”. From January to May 2027 I will be a visiting student researcher in the Department of Economics at UC Berkeley, with Prof. J. Bradford DeLong as faculty sponsor.

My research examines sovereign debt, financial markets, and political institutions across the long 19th century and the first age of globalisation, with a focus on how fiscal crises reshape legislative coalitions and borrowing costs. I combine archival research with computational text analysis, network methods, and panel econometrics. I also work on applied AI and labour-market research through the LSE Data Science Institute and the Hellenic Observatory. I am also an adviser at Scroll Home, a video-first property app for London, through an LSE Research in Practice placement (August to November 2026). The app was released on the UK App Store on 17 November 2026. Before academia, I worked in venture capital (Creandum, Speedinvest) and operations (Clausematch, Genie).

Research

Publications

Made in Greece or Echoed from Europe?

International Sentiment in the Tsouderos Archive and Kathimerini, 1931–1932

LLM / NLP · OCR Pipeline · Bai–Perron · Kathimerini · Tsouderos Archive

Forthcoming, Jahrbuch für Wirtschaftsgeschichte Download PDF

Abstract

Did international sentiment toward Greece shift before the April 1932 default, and if so, when? An LLM reading of 814 pages from the Tsouderos Archive (correspondence through the Bank of Greece with foreign officials) and 2,283 pages of Kathimerini dates the turn to May 1931, after the Kreditanstalt collapse, with further breaks around June and the September Sterling Crisis. Archive sentiment stays depressed through the autumn; topic composition shifts in November–December 1931 toward debt relief and financial assistance. Diplomatic correspondence reacted earlier and more abruptly than the domestic press.

Thesis Projects

Sterling by Design

Contractual Standardisation and Currency Dominance on the London Stock Exchange, 1869–1929

Security-Level Panel · Contract Standardisation · Currency Dominance · Bai-Perron Breaks · Government of India Loans

Draft Slides (PDF)

Abstract

Uses a security-level panel of 1,267 sovereign and colonial debt securities from the Investor's Monthly Manual. The paper shows sterling's share of income flows rose from 88 to 99 per cent as multi-currency clauses, bullion clauses, and non-sterling denominations disappeared. Breaks in contract terms begin in the early 1880s, ahead of several borrowers' gold adoption, and gold status shows no detectable association with sterling denomination. Within the Government of India, sterling loans yielded about 34 bp less than matched rupee loans.

Sovereign Debt Seniority and the Reintegration of Peripheral Borrowers

Evidence from International Financial Supervision in the Ottoman Empire, Egypt, and Greece, 1870 to 1914

Creditor Supervision · Junior-Senior Spreads · State-Contingent Coupons · Law of Control, 1898 · Seniority Reversal

Draft Slides (PDF)

Abstract

A defaulted sovereign needs new money to return, new money needs priority, and the holders already in place pay part of the cost. Under the Law of Control of 1898 the 2.5% Guaranteed Loan entered above the whole stack, while the old gold loans were ranked in place: the senior Monopoly Loan of 1887 yielded more than the junior Rentes of 1889 in 65 of 96 months in 1900 to 1907, against none of 35 months before default. Egypt after 1880 and the Ottoman Empire after 1881 are the comparisons. Presented at the 6th Macrohistory Conference, Bonn, September 2026.

Permeable Patronage

Fiscal Crises and Domain-Specific Defection in the Greek Parliament, 1875–1905

Roll-Call Dataset · Fiscal Flash Points · π-pattern / π-binding · Hellenic Parliament

Draft Slides (PDF)

Abstract

Builds a new roll-call dataset from digitised Hellenic Parliament proceedings: 755 nominal divisions, 132,246 MP-vote observations, and 1,049 matched MPs. The paper shows patronage discipline remained the default but became permeable at constituency-targeting fiscal flash points after default, and formalises the mechanism through a π-pattern/π-binding decomposition.

Cheap Talk on the Periphery

Greek Parliamentary Debates on Gold Standard Adoption, 1875–1885

988 Sittings · Forced Circulation · Bank Conventions · Hellenic Parliament · Gold Standard

Draft Slides (PDF)

Abstract

Reads 988 sittings of the Hellenic Parliament alongside the loan and bank statutes in the gazette. From 1877 forced circulation was a conditional state: the bank conventions tied its duration to the state's debt to the National and Ionian Banks and named no instrument to end it. The 1884 loan law listed the lift as a use of the proceeds without fixing a day, and Trikoupis told the chamber it would follow automatically once the money was deposited. Convertibility was in effect a term of the state's borrowing from its own banks. The chamber divided on loans, timing, and the bank conventions, but no deputy tabled a bill, amendment, or interpellation on gold, silver, or bimetallism as a regime. Presented at the WEast Workshop, Belgrade, August 2026.

Whatever Happened to Social History?

Social History in the Work of the Economic History Society, 1926–2026 (with Prof. Sir Rick Trainor)

EHS Council Minutes · AGM Records · Economic History Review · Conference Programmes

Draft, November 2026 Download PDF

Abstract

Examines the EHS’s century-long relationship with social history using un-digitised Council minutes, AGM records, and sampled Economic History Review articles and conference programmes (1927 to 2019). Finds thematic persistence of social topics (labour, demography, health, gender) alongside a methodological shift toward quantitative methods; the journal and conference diverged in approach while converging on the same subjects. Presented at the EHS Centenary Conference (April 2026).

A Predictable Crisis?

Deposit Losses during the German Banking Crisis of 1931 (with Philip Fliers, Queen’s University Belfast)

Bank-Level Panel · Logit Regressions · Funding Structure · German Banking Crisis

Draft, June 2026 Download PDF

Abstract

Why did severe deposit losses extend beyond the banks that failed in 1931? Using bimonthly German bank balance sheets (1925 to 1934; 8,616 bank-month observations), we show that vulnerability to large losses was highly predictable as early as 1927, that losses were comparable at surviving and failed banks, and that funding structure was the most consistent predictor, with timing differing by maturity. Even as aggregate deposits contracted, some banks gained deposits, and those inflows were themselves predictable from pre-crisis balance sheets.
